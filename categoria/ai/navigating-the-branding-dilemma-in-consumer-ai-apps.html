Navigating the Branding Dilemma in Consumer AI Apps

Google's Gemini has become the clearest example yet of an industry-wide problem — as TechCrunch detailed in August 2026, AI companies keep exposing their internal product architecture to users instead of hiding it.

In today's fast-evolving AI landscape, the challenge of branding artificial intelligence products has become impossible to ignore — and Google's Gemini is Exhibit A. As TechCrunch reported in late August 2026, Gemini now contains at least three separately branded features — Chat, Spark, and Daily Brief — each with its own icon, its own navigation space, and its own set of expectations for users trying to simply get something done.

Where Gemini's Branding Breaks Down

Daily Brief, meant to deliver "proactive, personalized updates," has drawn criticism for blurring the line between genuinely useful alerts and unwanted nudges — including resurfacing users' past searches in ways TechCrunch described as feeling "creepy" rather than helpful. Spark, an agentic feature capable of taking autonomous action on a user's behalf, is useful in principle but is branded as a standalone product rather than folded invisibly into a single interface — forcing users to learn what each piece is for before they can use any of them.

Google's own messaging undercuts itself here: the company has stated, "You shouldn't have to guess whether a task requires Spark, a Daily Brief, or a quick inbox search" — a promise that the existence of three distinctly named, separately branded features directly contradicts.

Not Just a Google Problem

The branding confusion extends well beyond Gemini. TechCrunch's reporting points to Anthropic's Claude, which splits its interface between "Chat" and "Cowork," and OpenAI's ChatGPT, which similarly asks users to move between "Chat" and "Work" modes. In each case, the underlying issue is the same: engineering-driven product design is exposing internal architecture — the seams between different AI capabilities — directly to consumers, rather than abstracting it away.

What a Better Approach Looks Like

By contrast, Apple's Siri strategy has generally avoided this pitfall by integrating AI capabilities into existing apps and workflows without requiring users to learn new product names or navigate between differently branded modes. As AI companies compete for mainstream adoption, the difference between a coherent, invisible AI layer and a maze of sub-brands may end up mattering as much as the underlying model quality itself — a lesson Gemini's rollout has made unusually visible.
