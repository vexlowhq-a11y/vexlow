Starship's Flight 13 Ship Achieves SpaceX's 'Softest Splashdown' Yet

The upper stage from SpaceX's July 24 test flight survived reentry intact and floated in the Indian Ocean for over two weeks before rough seas complicated recovery.

Leonardo Beltran·August 1, 2026·Updated September 9, 2026·7 min

What Happened, When

SpaceX launched Starship's 13th integrated test flight on July 24, 2026, at 6:51 p.m. EDT, carrying 20 next-generation Starlink V3 satellites (of a planned 60) as payload, according to Space.com. The Super Heavy booster had a hard landing, with only 5 of its 13 planned engines firing, and came down in the Gulf of Mexico. The Ship upper stage, by contrast, executed a controlled descent -- a three-engine burn to flip vertical, stepping down to two engines and then one -- and splashed down intact in the Indian Ocean.

The "Softest Splashdown"

"That is the softest splashdown we have ever had with Starship in the Indian Ocean," SpaceX spokesperson Dan Huot said during the flight's live broadcast. Elon Musk posted on X: "Starship is intact, floating in the ocean and transmitting telemetry!" It was the first time a Starship upper stage survived splashdown intact and airtight enough to float, rather than breaking apart on impact as on previous flights.

Why It Stayed Adrift for Weeks

Rough seas hampered recovery efforts, and the 171-foot vehicle remained afloat in the Indian Ocean for more than two weeks after splashdown, according to Gizmodo. SpaceX deployed a recovery vessel and two Norwegian tugboats, the Normand Ranger and Skimmer Tide, aiming to tow the Ship to Western Australia for inspection. Musk said the recovery was "not looking good" at points, but the extended float gave engineers, in Huot's words, "close-up photos of critical regions of the heat shield and engines for future upgrades" -- what SpaceX called "a dream scenario" for gathering heat-shield data.

Why This Matters

A Starship upper stage surviving reentry intact, rather than exploding, is a prerequisite for the reusability goals underpinning SpaceX's cost targets for Starlink deployment and, eventually, crewed missions.

Correcting the Record

The previous version of this article attributed its account to unnamed podcast hosts and never described what actually happened during the flight. This version replaces that framing with SpaceX's own on-record statements and named reporting on the flight and its aftermath.
